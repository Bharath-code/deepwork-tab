The problem

Every tab felt reasonable at the time.

Nobody opens forty of them on purpose. Each one was a good idea in the moment — and the pile is what's left of forty good ideas you never came back to.

How it works

Three steps, then it gets out of your way.

  1. Pick a number.

    Seven is the default. Most people are surprised how much room seven turns out to be.

  2. Open one too many, and a calm screen steps in.

    Not a block. Not a lecture. One question: what am I actually looking for?

  3. Queue it, or close something.

    Queued pages wait in the toolbar popup. Nothing is ever lost — that's the whole reason the queue exists.

Why it holds

Willpower is a bad interface. Friction is a good one.

Every tool with a settings page loses to the version of you that opens the settings page. So the settings here are deliberately lopsided:

Tightening the cap

Instant

Nine to seven takes effect the moment you let go of the slider. Good decisions shouldn't have to wait.

Loosening the cap

60 seconds

Raising it makes you sit with it for a minute. Long enough for the impulse to pass. Short enough that it's never a prison.

You will raise the cap sometimes. That's fine — it's your browser. The gate isn't there to stop you. It's there to make sure the decision is yours and not the itch's.

Honest answers

The questions people actually ask.

What if I actually need that tab?

Queue it. One key, and the title and URL are saved to the popup. Reopen it whenever — or export the whole queue as markdown links and paste it into your notes. The cap limits what's open, never what you can get back to.

Is this a website blocker?

No. There is no blocklist and no notion of a "bad" site. Research at 3pm and doomscrolling at 3am look identical to a blocklist; the difference is how many things you already had open. That's the thing this measures.

Does it watch my browsing?

No. Tab titles, URLs and everything you type stay in Chrome's local storage on your machine and are never transmitted. The extension makes exactly one network request: a daily ping containing a random install ID and today's date, so we know whether anyone still uses this. That's the entire data footprint, and the code is public.

What if seven is too few for how I work?

Then set it to twelve. The number isn't the point — having a number is. The default is a starting guess, not a judgement.

What stays free forever?

The cap, the intercept, the reason gate, the queue, recall, markdown export, the streak and the weekly receipt. Everything that builds the habit. Paying for the habit itself would mean the free version had to be worse at the only thing this product is for, and that's not a trade worth making.

Why no monthly plan?

Because the thing being sold takes months to work. A monthly plan invites you to judge it in week one, which is exactly when a habit feels like friction and nothing else. Annual matches the arc — and it's one renewal a year to think about instead of twelve.

What if Pro isn't worth it?

Fourteen days, no questions, no form. Reply to the receipt and you get your money back. And since nothing is charged until the features actually ship, the worst case for reserving today is an email you ignore.

Will it nag me?

Never. No notifications, no badges, no points, no leaderboards, no re-engagement emails, no streak you'll feel bad about breaking. An anti-distraction tool that fights for your attention is a contradiction, and those mechanics are on a permanent do-not-build list.
